The Biggest Sun Safety Myths We Heard

By Hanna Zhang

This week, when we asked people what they wanted to learn more about through our surveys, 35.2% picked sunscreen myths. That’s more than one in three people. So, we picked two of the most common ones to take a look at.

To start off, we found that sun safety information was most commonly found on social media at 41.8%. Family or friends were next at 23.1%, then healthcare professionals at 14.3% and school at 8.8%. Another 9.9% said they don’t usually look for sun safety information at all. So most people are learning about sunscreen from social media or from people they know. Both can be helpful but also inaccurate, and that is how myths can spread in our communities.

Myth 1: “I only need sunscreen at the beach.” This isn’t true. UV rays reach your skin any time you’re outside. These small amounts of sun don’t feel like much, but they add up over days and years. Our results show that 75.9% of people rated daily sun protection a 4 or 5 out of 5, and 47.3% gave it a 5. But only 26.4% said they always wear sunscreen when they’re outdoors. So people agree sun protection matters every day, yet many still do not use them. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher on any day you’ll be outside. Broad-spectrum means it protects against both UVA and UVB rays.

Myth 2: “It’s cloudy, so UV isn’t strong.” This is also false. Clouds make the day feel cooler and darker, so it seems like the sun can’t hurt you. But clouds block heat and brightness better than they block UV. The Skin Cancer Foundation says up to 80% of UV rays can pass through clouds. That’s why you can get burned on a gray day without ever feeling hot. 75.8% of the people we surveyed have been sunburned at least once, and it’s likely that some of those burns happened on days that didn’t seem sunny.

Instead of going by how the weather looks or feels, we should all check the UV Index. Most weather apps show it, and it tells you how strong the UV is at a certain place and time. When the number is 3 or higher, you should be using sun protection, and that can happen even on a cloudy day.

Lastly, if you see a sunscreen tip online and want to share it, check it first on the AAD, CDC, or Skin Cancer Foundation websites. If you can’t find it there, it’s better not to share it with others!

Sources: American Academy of Dermatology (AAD), Skin Cancer Foundation, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
